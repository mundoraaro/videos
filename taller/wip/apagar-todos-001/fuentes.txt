- EarthSky, "How many stars could you see on a clear moonless night?": las estimaciones van hasta ~10,000 estrellas visibles en todo el cielo; "around 2,000, the most common number you'll see for these estimates" → 'dos mil estrellas' en una noche oscura y despejada.
- Sky & Telescope (Bob King), "Ten thousand stars bedazzle the eye on a dark night. Wait, how many stars in the sky?": 9,096 estrellas a simple vista en todo el cielo (4,548 por hemisferio); con el límite de ciudad (magnitud +2) "just 70 stars worldwide, or 35 stars visible from say, downtown Chicago or Boston" → 'unas treinta y cinco' desde el centro de una ciudad grande. Cálculo: 2,000 ÷ 35 ≈ 57 → 'más de cincuenta veces más'.
- The Conversation (Curious Kids), "How do my eyes adjust to the dark, and how long does it take?": los conos se adaptan en ~5 min, los bastones toman el control a los ~10 min y "After about 20 minutes, your rods will be doing their best" → 'unos veinte minutos'.
- Science News (10 jun 2016), "A third of the population can't see the Milky Way at night" (Falchi et al., Science Advances 2016, New World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness): "for more than a third of Earth's population, the glare of artificial lights conceals this cosmic wonder"; "Nearly 80 percent of North Americans ... can no longer see the galactic core at night". CU Boulder Today (10 jun 2016): "one third of humanity".
- National Park Service, Dry Tortugas, "Sea Turtles and Light": las crías van "toward the brightest horizon, which was the light from the sky reflecting off the ocean"; con luces artificiales "the hatchlings may travel inland, toward the artificial lights" o "crawl onto roadways". FWC Florida, "Sea Turtle Disorientations": "Sometimes hatchlings crawl into the road".
- Discover Magazine (3 nov 2016), "For Our Own Good, Let There Be Dark": "At 4:31 a.m. on Jan. 17, 1994, residents of Los Angeles were jolted awake by a 6.7-magnitude earthquake"; el apagón "prompted dozens of residents to call the city's Griffith Observatory because of something weird they saw in the night sky: the Milky Way"; E.C. Krupp (director): "People were puzzled and wondered if there was a connection between the earthquake and the strange sky." Physics Today (5 ene 2011, citando a Los Angeles Times): el terremoto de Northridge "knocked out most of the power" y el Observatorio Griffith "received many calls asking about 'the strange sky they had seen after the earthquake'".
